# OpenCaptain Research Report

## Overall judgement

The nearest existing work splits into three overlapping camps rather than one finished product: enterprise agents that inherit existing app permissions, meeting assistants that summarize and answer questions in real time, and research prototypes that try to make group discussion more inclusive, balanced, and deliberative. I did not find a public product that combines all of the behaviors you described: cross-team coordination, permission-inherited action across chat/meetings/tasks, strict communication etiquette, natural-language approvals for exceptions, and fully traceable knowledge/provenance over conflicting sources. That makes OpenCaptain feel less like a slight variation on today’s meeting bots and more like a new category at the intersection of enterprise agent governance, meeting facilitation, and organizational memory. citeturn17view3turn17view4turn27view1turn27view2turn18view4turn18view5

Your earlier A/B/C knowledge model fits this problem unusually well. In this domain, the hard part is not merely retrieving documents; it is deciding what the agent is allowed to believe, say, and do, under which authority, at what time, and with what audit trail. That is exactly where provenance standards, risk-management guidance, and the newer “graph memory” literature are pointing: keep sources and derivations explicit, version beliefs, and make the memory itself inspectable rather than reconstructing rationales after the fact. citeturn28view6turn28view5turn28view4turn28view1turn28view0

The strongest strategic conclusion is that OpenCaptain should be designed first as a governed coordination actor and only second as a generative assistant. The market already knows how to build note-taking assistants and chat Q&A. The differentiator in your concept is policy-constrained participation, human approval for boundary crossings, and cross-silo coordination with evidence-backed memory. citeturn9search1turn17view0turn18view4turn28view7

## What the research says about meetings, breakouts, and AI facilitation

There is real evidence behind your intuition that smaller groups make participation easier. A large remote-meeting study across five organizations found that vocal participation was the strongest predictor of inclusiveness and effectiveness, that small meetings were associated with increased participation, and that every two additional participants corresponded to about a one-point absolute drop in meeting effectiveness and inclusiveness ratings. Separate discussion research has likewise found higher participation in small-group settings and more equal participation across different types of participants; small-group workshop work has also found that moving directly to a full-group discussion produced the least participation, while small groups produced the most. citeturn26view0turn24search0turn24search8turn24search15

That supports your instinct for a “small-meeting exception,” but it does **not** justify any particular magic number such as 2. The literature supports the direction of the rule, not the exact threshold. In practice, that means OpenCaptain’s exception threshold should be a configurable policy that you test against real meeting telemetry, not a supposedly universal constant. citeturn26view0turn24search0turn24search15

The “raise hand before speaking” idea is also more than etiquette theater. Microsoft researchers found that virtual raise-hand use was associated with improved meeting inclusiveness, but the feature was used in less than 1% of meetings they examined; they proposed nudging people to use it when the system detected failed interruptions. Mainstream platforms already expose the necessary interaction primitive: teams, webinars, and conference platforms support raised hands, order notifications, and moderator-controlled speaking. That gives OpenCaptain a practical compliance path: it can use existing floor-acquisition norms instead of inventing its own signaling system. citeturn12view2turn16search0turn16search1turn16search2

On the group-process side, the most relevant classic family of methods is structured consensus. The nominal group technique and Delphi method are both designed for settings where evidence is incomplete or views conflict, and nominal-group approaches explicitly use structure to encourage contributions and reduce domination. That matters because your agent is partly a coordination role and partly a conflict-buffer. If OpenCaptain is moving information between groups, it should not behave like an unconstrained conversationalist; it should behave like a structured facilitator that knows when an item is still a contradiction, when it has become a proposal, and when it has become an approved decision. citeturn25search1turn25search0turn25search2

The recent AI facilitation literature is strikingly aligned with that conclusion. A 2025 meeting-inclusion study proposed an “Observe, Ask, Intervene” pattern and found that people preferred the agent asking questions before intervening rather than acting fully autonomously. Another 2025 group-chat study found that participants valued an AI teammate, but disliked it when the agent dominated discussion and wanted controls over when, what, and where it responded. A 2026 study of an AI agent mediating feedback on meeting inclusion improved speaking-time balance in the lab, but the field deployment revealed organizational barriers and pushed usage toward reflection rather than direct social correction. In other words: the research favors helpful, bounded, controllable intervention over agentic assertiveness. citeturn23view7turn12view0turn13search3

The most directly relevant evidence for your breakout-and-cross-pollination idea is newer and still early, but promising. Research on AI-mediated deliberation found that AI-generated mediator statements were seen as more impartial and left groups less divided. A 2026 ideation system called GraftMind let people work in private while an AI mediator selectively shared transformed ideas across participants; compared with conventional group brainstorming, users generated more ideas, higher-originality ideas, reported more synergy, and felt less evaluation apprehension. Related work on LLM-powered “devil’s advocate” systems shows that AI can help surface minority viewpoints or provoke deeper deliberation without requiring a human participant to bear the social cost of dissent. These findings do not yet prove that enterprise “agents listening across breakouts and parroting points” is production-ready, but they do suggest that your direction is scientifically plausible, especially when the AI mediates and reframes rather than simply blurting out raw transcript fragments. citeturn13search2turn13search8turn30view0turn31search0turn31search3

Finally, the organizational coordination literature also points toward your “cross-team scrum lead” framing. Research on inter-team coordination in large-scale agile work treats cross-team synchronization as a distinct coordination problem rather than a mere byproduct of local standups. Work on transactive memory systems and information elaboration makes a similar point from another angle: teams do better when members know where expertise resides and when functionally diverse knowledge is actually exchanged and integrated, especially in turbulent environments. OpenCaptain fits naturally into that space as a mechanism for transactive memory, coordination, and information elaboration across silos. citeturn14search27turn14search3turn15search0turn23view3

## What the market still does not solve well

The reviewed products are strong on permission inheritance, summarization, search, and workflow automation, but weak on social protocol. I did not find a widely deployed enterprise product that says, in effect, “this agent will remain silent in a meeting unless called on, will only initiate outreach inside specified group boundaries, and will require a human natural-language approval before crossing those boundaries.” The market has elements of that design, but mostly as separate pieces: approval cards in chat, admin toggles, role-based scopes, or workflow builders. The integrated etiquette layer you want is largely absent. citeturn18view6turn18view7turn20view0turn17view1turn17view8

That absence is not accidental. The research literature repeatedly warns that proactive agent participation in groups can become disruptive, socially awkward, or norm-violating if the humans cannot control it. Commercial vendors appear to have responded by keeping most workplace agents in a safer lane: summarize, search, recommend, and maybe automate a bounded workflow, but do not behave like an autonomous junior manager. OpenCaptain’s central challenge is therefore partly product design and partly governance design: how do you let an agent coordinate across silos without turning it into an uninvited participant in everyone’s social space. citeturn12view0turn23view7turn13search3turn9search1turn17view0

The other major gap is contradiction handling. Today’s enterprise assistants are usually optimized for retrieval and synthesis from what a user can access, not for maintaining explicit competing claims with different authority levels over time. Your project requires a stronger epistemic model because it must decide whether a meeting transcript merely contradicts an existing plan, whether a manager’s statement supersedes it, whether a ticket update acknowledges and replaces an older commitment, and whether an approval message grants temporary authority for an otherwise forbidden communication. That is exactly where standard RAG begins to fray and where a versioned, provenance-rich knowledge layer becomes valuable. citeturn17view3turn17view5turn28view0turn28view1

## How the knowledge model fits OpenCaptain

For OpenCaptain, layer A should be the evidence layer: raw messages, meeting transcripts, tickets, documents, calendar events, task updates, approval messages, ACL snapshots, hand-raise events, invite events, and human decisions. This layer should be append-oriented and versioned, not overwritten, because later governance questions will often be temporal: what did the agent know, what was it told, and what approval existed **at that time**. Provenance standards from **entity["organization","W3C","web standards body"]** are directly useful here because they model entities, activities, people, derivation, and versioning for trust and interchange rather than forcing you into ad hoc lineage fields. citeturn28view6turn28view5

Layer B should be the claims-and-relations layer: extracted commitments, task states, participant roles, decisions, risks, dependencies, ownership, approval status, and contradiction/supersession/support links. This is where your suggested distinction between “clear acknowledgment and replacement” versus “mere contradiction” becomes essential. B is not just a bag of triples; it needs typed edges such as *supported_by*, *contradicted_by*, *supersedes*, *approved_by*, *expires_at*, and *applies_to_scope*. Temporal KG systems are already moving in this direction. Zep/Graphiti, for example, stores raw episodes separately from semantic entities and maintains time-aware relationships rather than flattening everything into a static fact store. citeturn28view0turn28view3

Layer C still matters, but it should be treated as a view layer rather than the canonical memory. In your earlier framing, C can be split into temporary runtime views and persistent situational views. For OpenCaptain, that is the right move. A meeting-specific “what I believe right now for this audience and this agenda” view can be computed on demand and cached, but if that view causes an external action, such as messaging another team or summarizing a dispute, it should itself become an object with lineage back to the exact A and B versions used. Newer graph-memory work makes this point explicitly: the goal is not just long context, but versioned beliefs, provenance edges, dependency tracking, and immutable audit trails around what an agent concluded and why. citeturn28view1turn28view2

This is especially important for approvals and authority. A natural-language message like “yes, OpenCaptain can coordinate with finance on this thread” should not be treated as free-floating text after it is parsed. It should create a governed object in A, then a scoped authorization claim in B, with the approver identity, approved action, scope, duration, and any required observers attached. In that framing, “approved by person X” is not merely an opinion-like attribute; it is a first-class governance fact whose semantics matter operationally. That is one of the best reasons **not** to collapse everything fully into one undifferentiated graph. citeturn18view6turn20view0turn28view7turn28view4

So yes: the knowledge model you described incorporates very well into this paradigm, and in fact solves one of the biggest weaknesses of the current market. The only caveat is to avoid over-materializing unstable interpretations. Keep A rich and durable, keep B explicit and versioned, and let C be generated per context unless the view itself becomes action-relevant or review-relevant. citeturn28view0turn28view1turn28view6

## Architecture implications for OpenCaptain

The safest architecture is identity-first and least-privilege by default. OpenCaptain should exist as a first-class app or service principal in each host system, but user-initiated actions should usually run through delegated permissions rather than broad app-only authority. Microsoft Graph’s model is explicit that delegated permissions let the app act on behalf of a signed-in user and cannot exceed that user’s underlying access, whereas application permissions can access all data granted to the app role. Slack’s platform guidance similarly pushes least privilege and warns against over-broad scopes. Atlassian says scopes do not override product permissions. Put plainly: for OpenCaptain, technical scope should be the outer boundary, and source-system ACLs should be the inner boundary that actually decides what the agent may see or do. citeturn18view10turn20view2turn18view11

Meeting behavior should follow the same philosophy. A good default policy is precisely the research-backed one: observe first, ask or request the floor second, intervene third. In large or mixed-status meetings, the agent should raise its hand, wait to be called on, and then speak briefly. In very small meetings, the policy engine may allow freer turn-taking, but that should be configurable and host-visible. The availability of raised-hand controls and moderator signaling in mainstream meeting platforms means this is implementable without requiring a proprietary interface. citeturn23view7turn12view2turn16search0turn16search1turn16search2

Communication boundaries should be expressed as policy over existing organizational groups, not as a separate contact graph invented by the agent. Your whitelisted team DL and admin DL idea fits that model well. The higher-safety implementation, in my judgment, is that any message outside the whitelisted team scope creates a pending action object, resolves the intended recipients against source-system groups, requests approval in-channel or via a structured approval object, and only then executes. Even if the human approval arrives as natural language, the approved action should be normalized into a structured authorization record before the outbound message is sent. That pattern is more consistent with how enterprises already manage approvals in chat and more auditable than letting the agent infer authority from vague prose alone. citeturn18view6turn18view7turn20view0turn28view7

The contradiction policy is where your graph model becomes essential. OpenCaptain should never silently replace one claim with another unless it has a typed basis for doing so: explicit supersession language, a higher-authority source, a later approved decision, or a reconciled human judgment. Otherwise, it should preserve competing claims and present them as such. That is particularly important when the agent moves information across silos, because a coordination agent that overstates certainty can create organizational damage faster than a passive assistant can. The agent’s public outputs should therefore carry lightweight epistemic labels such as *current approved position*, *pending contradiction*, *unverified meeting assertion*, or *awaiting human adjudication*. citeturn28view6turn28view1turn28view4

The final design verdict is that OpenCaptain is feasible, but only if you resist the temptation to make it “smart everywhere” before making it “governed everywhere.” The literature supports smaller-group facilitation, structured deliberation, and AI-mediated cross-pollination. The product market supports permission inheritance, approvals in chat, meeting attendance by assistants, and cross-app search/automation. What remains open is the productization of polite, authority-aware, contradiction-aware participation across teams. That gap is exactly where your A/B/C knowledge model gives the project an edge rather than extra complexity. citeturn26view0turn30view0turn17view3turn17view5turn18view4turn28view1
